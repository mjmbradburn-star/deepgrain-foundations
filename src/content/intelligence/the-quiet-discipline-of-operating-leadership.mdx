## The confusion between volume and impact

Walk into most leadership team meetings and you can tell within ten minutes who the org thinks is "strong." It's the person who reframes the debate, who has a line ready for every objection, who leaves the room having visibly moved something. It reads well. It photographs well. Boards like it. New hires quote it back in exit interviews six months later.

None of that tells you whether the business is better run.

I've sat across the table from operators who never do any of it. They ask three questions in a meeting and leave. They don't reframe anything, because the frame was already correct before they walked in. Six months later the retention numbers have moved, the roadmap has shipped on schedule, and nobody in the building can point to the moment it happened, because there wasn't one moment. There were fifty small ones, none of which needed witnesses.

Loud and effective are different axes. They correlate less than most people assume, and in operating roles, where the job is running the machine rather than selling the vision of the machine, they can be close to inversely correlated. The volume is often compensating for something. Certainty that hasn't been tested. A decision that needs an audience to survive contact with reality.

## What quiet actually means

Quiet is not the same as passive or conflict-avoidant. Some of the quietest operators I know are also the most direct people in the building, they just don't perform the directness. They say the hard thing once, to the person who needs to hear it, and they don't need the room.

The distinction is where the energy goes. Loud leadership spends energy on the announcement: the all-hands moment, the Slack message with three exclamation marks, the framing of the decision as a Decision. Quiet leadership spends the same energy on the decision itself and on what happens after it, when nobody's watching to see if it holds.

A carpenter doesn't argue with wood. They read it first. A quiet operator reads the org the same way, they notice where the grain runs before they start cutting, and most of their real work happens before anyone else in the room knows there was a question on the table.

## Cadence is the tell

Here's the practical test I use with clients: strip away every meeting where something is being announced, and look at what's left. Is there still a rhythm? A 1:1 that happens on the same Thursday whether the quarter is going well or badly. A retro that runs whether or not there's a juicy incident to dissect. A weekly written update that goes out at 5pm regardless of whether there's good news to lead with.

That residual cadence, the stuff that happens with nothing to announce, is the actual discipline. It's boring to watch and it's the single best predictor I have of whether a leader will still be effective in eighteen months. Leaders who only show up loud, for launches and crises and town halls, are leaders whose system depends on drama to function. Take away the drama and the system stalls, because the drama was doing the work the cadence should have been doing.

I've watched this fail in real time. A CPO client had built a genuinely good comms rhythm around big moments: reorg announcements, comp cycle reveals, offsite keynotes. All well executed. But between those moments, nothing. No standing forum, no written signal, nothing that told the org "the person steering this is still steering it." The org filled the silence with its own story, and the story was always worse than the truth. The fix wasn't more communication. It was less performance and more cadence: the same short update, same day, same format, every week, whether or not there was news.

## Decisions made and not announced

The most underrated leadership behaviour I know is making a real decision and not telling anyone it happened. Not hiding it, that's different and it's a failure mode. I mean deciding to kill a project, or change a reporting line, or walk back a policy, and just doing it through the normal channels, without a moment being manufactured around it.

Announced decisions carry a cost most leaders don't price in: they create a commitment to the announcement, not just the decision. Once you've stood up and declared the new direction, reversing it costs face, and face-costs bend judgement. Quiet decisions don't carry that tax. They can be revised the following week if the data says so, because nobody built a story around them that now needs protecting.

This is uncomfortable for leaders who've been trained, correctly, that visibility drives influence. There's a real tension here: total silence reads as absence, and absence is its own failure. The answer is to communicate the outcomes people actually need (what changed, who it affects, what to do differently) and skip the theatre around how the decision came to be. Nobody downstream needs to watch the sausage get made. They need the sausage, on time, and they need to trust that the next one will show up too.

## What this means in practice

If you're trying to build this discipline rather than just admire it from a distance, start with the cadence, not the charisma. Pick the one recurring forum that has the least drama attached to it, the update nobody's watching closely, and protect it absolutely. Run it in the weeks nothing's happening. Especially in the weeks nothing's happening.

Then audit your own decisions for the last quarter. How many were announced before they were tested? How many needed an audience to feel real? That ratio is a more honest read on your leadership than any 360 review will give you, because a 360 measures how you're perceived, and perception is exactly the thing that quiet leadership is willing to sacrifice for the sake of getting the work right.

- Optimise for signal, not volume. The team you trust most is the one that hears you least often, but always for something real.
- Keep the cadence even when nothing dramatic is happening. The cadence is the discipline.
- Decisions made and not announced are still decisions. Sometimes the right ones.
