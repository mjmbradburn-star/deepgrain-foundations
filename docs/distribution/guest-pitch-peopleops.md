## Draft article (attached)

### The five questions to ask before approving an AI pilot in People

Most People AI pilots are killed by the same five questions, asked too late.

The pilot demos beautifully in October. By January, it's quietly dead. Trace it back and the failure isn't the model. It's that the approval meeting in September skipped these five questions.

Ask them before you sign off and the pilot has a fighting chance. Skip them and you're funding a clever demo that won't survive the move from your desk to your team's inbox.

**1. Who owns this workflow on the Monday after launch?**

A pilot has a project manager. Production needs an operating leader. The project manager will be on to the next thing by Q1; the workflow needs an owner who'll sit with it for the year. If you can't name that person at approval, you're not designing a pilot. You're funding a one-off.

**2. Where does the data come from, on what schedule, with whose permission?**

Pilots tend to run on clean exports prepared by a person on the team. Production needs the data to flow on its own, on a schedule, with permissions and refresh and someone owning it when it breaks. Ask the data question at approval and the answer often reveals the pilot wasn't actually planned for production at all.

**3. What does the agent call, and how is it logged and reversed?**

If the integration is a screenshot, a copy-paste, or a personal API key, it doesn't survive the people who built it leaving. Production needs a stable interface, a logged audit trail, and a permissions model the org actually owns. The pilot doesn't need to be perfect here, but the production version does, and the pilot should be able to point to where the difference will live.

**4. What's the written policy on what the agent must not decide?**

Governance isn't the brake. It's the steering. The People teams that move fastest with AI are the ones that decided early what they would never let it decide. Write the "never" list before the pilot starts. It's shorter than people fear, and it makes every later decision faster.

**5. What forum reviews this workflow's drift, and how often?**

An AI workflow without a recurring review degrades silently. The output rejection rate creeps up. The operators stop trusting it. Six months later, the workflow is officially live and operationally dead. Pick a forum at approval. Cadence is the cheapest pillar to build and the most expensive to skip.

Ask these five at approval. The answers don't all have to be perfect; they have to be honest. A pilot whose answers are "we'll figure it out later" hasn't been designed yet. A pilot whose answers are "here, here, here, here, and here" is already most of the way to production.
